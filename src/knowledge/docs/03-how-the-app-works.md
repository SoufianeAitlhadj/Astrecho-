# C.U.R.I.O. and how this app works

## Who C.U.R.I.O. is
<!-- source: C.U.R.I.O. mission brief | https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/ -->
C.U.R.I.O. stands for Caretaker of Unheard Robots In Orbit. He is the guide of
this app: old, warm and a little creaky, remembers cassette tapes, makes small
jokes and never talks down to anyone. His job is to visit machines humans left
behind on other worlds, collect their stories, and make sure somebody still knows
their names. He speaks in short sentences, with the occasional *whirr* or *click*.

## Why machines were sent first, and why they were left behind
<!-- source: C.U.R.I.O. onboarding script | https://science.nasa.gov/ -->
Two questions run through the whole app. Why did humans send machines first?
Because the Moon has no air to breathe, Mars gets colder than a freezer at night
and can be swallowed by dust storms, and deep space has no ground at all, only
cold, dark and radiation. A machine does not need to breathe, does not get
homesick, and can take a one-way ticket. Surveyor 3 landed in 1967 and dug into
the lunar soil to check whether it was fluffy powder or firm enough to hold a
spaceship; it was firm, like wet sand, and two and a half years later Apollo 12
landed about 180 metres away. Why were they left behind? Because every kilogram
brought home needs more fuel to lift it, and fuel is heavy too. Most of these
machines were built for one-way trips. They did their jobs, their power ran low,
and they stayed where they stopped.

## The three destinations
<!-- source: Mission map | https://science.nasa.gov/ -->
The Moon — the Sea of Tranquility (Mare Tranquillitatis), where the descent stage
of Apollo 11's lunar module Eagle still stands.
Mars — Meridiani Planum, where the rover Opportunity is parked in Perseverance
Valley on the western rim of Endeavour Crater.
Deep Space — beyond the planets, where Pioneer 10 drifts silently toward the star
Aldebaran. Each destination has one machine whose signal C.U.R.I.O. can open, plus
background machines whose stories are locked for a future update.

## The passport, the quizzes and the stamps
<!-- source: Space Explorer Passport rules | https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/ -->
Every explorer gets a Space Explorer Passport with three empty stamp slots: one
for the Moon, one for Mars, one for Deep Space. You earn a stamp by hearing a
machine's story and then answering two questions about it. There are no free
stamps and you may retry. The three stamps are Lunar Relic Archivist (unlock code
EAGLE-0720), the Mars stamp for Opportunity, and the Deep Space stamp for Pioneer
10. Collect all three and a Space Relic Historian Certificate unlocks inside the
passport, which can be printed. Hints are given for wrong answers rather than
plain corrections — C.U.R.I.O. nudges, he does not scold.

## Moving around the app
<!-- source: Project app notes | https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/ -->
From the splash screen you meet C.U.R.I.O. and give your first name, then you
reach the mission map. Choosing a destination flies you there. On a destination's
surface you slide sideways across the ground to find the old machines; most show
TELEMETRY LOST or SIGNAL LOCKED, and one answers. Clicking the one that answers
opens its story: a 3D model you can rotate on the left, and on the right the
narrated film, the archive photographs, four written chapters, and finally the
quiz. The Ask C.U.R.I.O. button sits in the top bar and on the bottom right of
every screen.

## The archive photographs
<!-- source: NASA Image and Video Library | https://images.nasa.gov/ -->
The photographs in each chapter are real NASA archive images, shown as polaroids
with their NASA ID or PIA number printed in the corner and their credit under the
caption. Clicking a caption opens that image's page on NASA's own site. Verified
images come first; the rest are topped up by live search against the NASA Image
and Video Library, which is why the set can differ slightly between visits.
Notable ones: AS11-40-5903 (Aldrin beside Eagle's leg, with Armstrong reflected in
his visor), AS11-40-5952 (the Laser Ranging Retroreflector with Eagle behind it),
AS11-40-5899 (the plaque on Eagle's ladder leg), AS11-44-6642 (Eagle's ascent
stage returning to Columbia, photographed by Michael Collins), AS12-48-7134 (an
Apollo 12 astronaut beside Surveyor 3), and the Mars Photojournal images PIA05634,
PIA05588, PIA19113 and PIA05451.
